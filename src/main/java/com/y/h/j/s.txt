In our modern world, where online stores and digital subscriptions dominate, the significance of physical presence seems secondary. Many argue that to save costs and achieve efficiency, companies should automate processes, ship products directly, and close factories and physical stores. This makes sense from an accounting perspective, as it can account for significant profit growth. However, I argue that this view is flawed because it treats human connection as insignificant.

Consider a local ceramic workshop. A novice might use an electric kiln to produce functional wares, a process that appears predictable. Yet, the truest value is not in the ceramic pot itself, but in the experience. When a master, with sincere intent, instructs a cheerful intern on how to shape clay into an elegant jar, that moment of learning and shared effort is tangible. This cannot be automated or replaced by browsing an online image. A photograph of a bowl on a website lacks the atmosphere of the workshop, the smell of the kiln, and the casual comment of a fellow artist. While a digital marketplace offers convenience, it often fails to provide the sense of community and the personal touch that local businesses, from art studios to bookshops, present.

Furthermore, the threat to these local enterprises is not just from online competition. When a government decides to charge high taxes or a city council lacks the will to support small businesses, entire communities suffer. The gradual disappearance of these spaces leads to a lack of social cohesion. Therefore, although investing in tech and digital solutions is important, we must also make an effort to invest in our local economies. After all, a healthy society is not only about economic capital but also about social capital—the relationships and shared experiences that form the very fabric of our towns and cities. We should not trade this human element for the cold efficiency of a fully automated system.
